# "Water positive" pledges are reported at 75-78% of consumption, with replenishment pooled across a global project portfolio

**Topic:** Greenwashing evidence — water-positive pledge accounting; watershed and timeframe matching
**Source:** Google 2026 Environmental Report; Amazon 2025 Sustainability Report
**Type:** corporate disclosure
**URL:** https://sustainability.google/files/google-2026-environmental-report.pdf
**Published:** 2026

## Finding

Neither Google nor Amazon is water positive, and both say so in their data while continuing to headline the pledge. Google replenished 7.7 billion gallons in 2025, equal to 78% of its freshwater consumption, against a 120%-by-2030 target; its freshwater consumption rose 37% in the same year. Amazon reports 75% progress toward its water-positive data centre goal, with a footnote stating that "a number below 100% indicates we are still working to meet the water positive goal"; its data centres withdrew 9.4 billion litres in 2025 and its replenishment projects returned 9.4 billion litres — but the returns come from 45 projects worldwide, not from the watersheds the withdrawals occurred in. Amazon separately reports that 22% of its data centre withdrawals were in regions of high or extremely high water stress. Both companies count replenishment volumes pooled across a global portfolio against consumption that is concentrated in specific, often stressed, basins.

## Methodology

Google uses Volumetric Water Benefit Accounting 2.0, quantified by a third party, and states it counts benefits only "from projects that are active within the watersheds that our operations rely on and that have confirmed volumetric benefits from the reporting year" — a within-year, within-watershed constraint that is stricter than the pooled-global reading. Google excludes seawater and reclaimed wastewater from the consumption denominator. Amazon measures progress by adding reused water, avoided water and replenished water against data centre use under its own Water Positive Methodology; its replenishment figure is a global sum and the report does not disclose the basin-level match between where water was withdrawn and where it was returned.

Both companies report large forward-looking replenishment capacities (Google 19.7 bn gallons by 2030; Amazon 18 bn litres annually once complete) that have not yet been delivered. Amazon's claim that its data centres withdraw "about one-seventh of what an average data center withdraws" is a comparison against an unnamed industry average.

## Limitations and conflicts

Self-reported, with company-defined methodologies. VWBA 2.0 is an established third-party framework and Google's stated watershed and timeframe constraints are the strongest of the two; the weakness is that 97 watersheds cannot cover every basin Google operates in, and the report does not disclose the per-basin balance. Amazon's methodology permits reused and avoided water to count toward "returning more water to communities than we use," which is a different physical claim than replenishment. Neither company's disclosure allows a reader to check whether replenishment occurred in the basins and seasons where depletion occurred. Both report the shortfall openly; the gap between the disclosed percentages and the marketed pledge is where the claim becomes misleading.
