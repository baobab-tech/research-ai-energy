# 87% of the offsets retired by the twenty largest corporate buyers carry a high risk of not representing real, additional reductions

**Topic:** Greenwashing evidence — quality of the carbon offsets behind corporate carbon-neutral claims
**Source:** Gregory Trencher, Sascha Nick, Jordan Carlson, Matthew Johnson
**Type:** peer-reviewed (Nature Communications 15:6863)
**URL:** https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-51151-w
**Published:** 2024-08

## Finding

Trencher and colleagues assembled a company-level dataset of every offset retirement made on the three largest voluntary registries by the twenty companies retiring the most credits between January 2020 and December 2023, then scored those credits against four published quality criteria. 87% of the credits fall into the high-risk category of the Stockholm Environment Institute and GHG Management Institute framework, meaning a high likelihood of overstating emissions reductions or lacking additionality, and only 6.0% are low-risk. Removals account for 2.3% of retirements; 97.7% are avoidance credits, dominated by forest conservation and renewable energy projects. All but one of the twenty companies used these credits to claim carbon neutrality or to pursue a decarbonisation target.

## Methodology

A census, not a sample: every retirement recorded on VCS, CDM and Gold Standard for the twenty highest-volume corporate retirers over four years, compiled into an original company-level dataset from registry records and the UC Berkeley Voluntary Registry Offsets Database. Credits were scored on four dimensions against externally published rules, none of them set by the authors: project-type risk from the SEI/GHGMI Quality Offsets Guide, age from the CORSIA 2016 vintage and start-year cutoff, price against Ecosystem Marketplace category averages, and, for renewable energy credits only, country of implementation against Gold Standard and VCS eligibility rules using World Bank income classifications and IRENA penetration data. The analysis is of credit attributes, not of project-level field verification, so it inherits the risk classifications of the frameworks it applies.

## Limitations and conflicts

The authors are at Kyoto University, EPFL and a third institution; they declare no competing interests and name no funder, thanking only unnamed expert reviewers. The study examines no technology, cloud or data-centre company. Its twenty firms are in energy (Shell, Chevron, Eni, Sasol, PetroChina), aviation (Delta, easyJet, Boeing), automotive (Audi, Volkswagen), shipping and cruise, logistics, pharmaceuticals, banking, telecoms, luxury goods and professional services. Data centres, artificial intelligence and cloud computing are not mentioned anywhere in the paper. Its bearing on AI infrastructure runs through the instrument, not the sector: it establishes the base rate of quality in the voluntary carbon market that any corporate carbon-neutral claim resting on retired credits must be read against. The quality scores are proxies for additionality, not measurements of it, and the price indicator infers quality from project category averages, not from transacted prices. Retirement volume identifies the largest buyers, which are not a representative sample of corporate offset users.

## Relation to existing corpus

Supplies the market-wide base rate against which washing/017 should be read: Microsoft's own 2026 disclosure states that its carbon-neutrality claims were supported in part by spot purchases of unbundled certificates and by "carbon removal credits from existing projects", and this study's age criterion is precisely the test that credits from existing projects fail. Distinct from washing/024 and washing/025, which concern Scope 2 renewable energy certificates. Certificates and voluntary-market offsets are separate instruments and a company can rely on both.
