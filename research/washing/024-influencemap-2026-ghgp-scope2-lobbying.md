# Cloud companies split on the GHG Protocol's Scope 2 revision: Google supported stricter rules, Amazon and Apple signed against, Microsoft and Meta filed nothing public

**Topic:** Greenwashing evidence — corporate lobbying on the accounting standard that produces "100% renewable" claims
**Source:** InfluenceMap, "Corporate Engagement on the GHG Protocol Scope 2 Consultation"; PracticalESG report on the 66-signatory opposition letter
**Type:** NGO analysis of consultation record
**URL:** https://influencemap.org/insight/Corporate-Engagement-on-the-GHG-Protocol-Scope-2-Consultation-39494
**Published:** 2026-08-28

## Finding

The GHG Protocol's draft Scope 2 revision — the first in over a decade — proposes mandatory hourly temporal matching and geographic deliverability for market-based claims, which would invalidate much of the certificate-based accounting the hyperscalers' "100% renewable" and "carbon neutral" claims rest on. InfluenceMap's review of the October 2025-January 2026 consultation found Google alone among the large technology companies filed a public response supporting the updates, stating "the proposed updates improve accuracy and scientific integrity." Apple, Amazon, Meta and Microsoft filed no public response. Amazon and Apple instead signed a late-March 2026 letter, with 66 signatories representing over 33,500 organisations, arguing the changes "fundamentally threaten participation in this voluntary market" and asking that hourly and deliverability matching be optional rather than mandatory. Of 1,072 consultation responses, over half were anonymous; the GHG Protocol's own summary records that companies and trade groups broadly opposed the updates while academic and civil-society respondents supported them at significantly higher rates.

## Methodology

InfluenceMap reviewed submitted consultation responses and public positions of entities in the utilities, technology and energy sectors with a documented history of Scope 2 engagement. Positions are classified by what each entity wrote about mandatory temporal correlation and deliverability. The anonymity of more than half of responses limits attribution: absence from the public record is not evidence a company filed nothing, only that it did not file publicly.

Hewlett Packard argued the hourly-matching provision should read "may" rather than "shall"; Rivian wrote that it "strongly disagree[s] with the proposal to mandate temporal correlation"; Workday opposed mandatory hourly and deliverability rules.

## Limitations and conflicts

InfluenceMap is an advocacy-oriented research nonprofit that scores corporate climate lobbying and has a stated position favouring stricter standards; the classification of positions is its own. The opposition letter's substantive argument is not frivolous: signatories contend that aggregating load across a wide area to underwrite a new renewable project is the highest-impact action available to voluntary buyers, and that an hourly-matching mandate would steer capital away from large long-term offtake. That argument is contested by the peer-reviewed literature (see `research/washing/025-bjorn-2025-stricter-scope2-rules.md`) but it is a real methodological dispute, not only a defence of favourable accounting. Google's support is consistent with its own disclosure practice, which already publishes hourly CFE and Granular Certificate data.
