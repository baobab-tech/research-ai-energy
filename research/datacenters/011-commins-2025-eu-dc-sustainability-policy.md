# The EU's 2030 climate-neutral data centre aim is a Commission strategy objective with no binding sustainability target behind it, only binding reporting

**Topic:** Policy and regulation — the gap between EU data-centre reporting duties and enforceable outcomes
**Source:** Jessica Commins and Kristina Irion, University of Amsterdam
**Type:** peer-reviewed (Technology and Regulation 2025.001, pp. 1-36)
**URL:** https://doi.org/10.71265/c1nnwh92
**Source note:** open PDF https://www.ivir.nl/publicaties/download/TechReg2025.001Commins.pdf
**Published:** 2025-03

## Finding

The EU objective of "climate-neutral, highly energy-efficient and sustainable data centres by no later than 2030" originates in the Commission's 2020 "Shaping Europe's Digital Future" communication and is a policy aim, not a legal obligation. Commins and Irion trace the instruments actually adopted in its pursuit and find they create binding reporting and transparency duties without any binding sustainability target: Article 12 of the recast Energy Efficiency Directive and Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2024/1364, adopted 14 March 2024 and in force from 6 June 2024, oblige operators above 500 kW installed IT power demand to report, while the EU Taxonomy and CSRD route disclosures to investors, not to regulators with enforcement powers. Their central argument is that further regulation should translate the reporting requirements into binding sustainability targets, because energy-efficiency measures alone cannot contain the sector's emissions once the rebound effect is admitted.

## Methodology

Legal and policy analysis. The authors read the EU instrument stack applying to data centres, covering the Energy Efficiency Directive, Renewable Energy Directive, Ecodesign rules, the EU Taxonomy Regulation and its delegated acts, the CSRD and CSDD, the AI Act and the non-binding Climate Neutral Data Centre Pact, alongside Commission communications, staff working documents and commissioned studies over the period assessed. No measurement is produced. Sectoral figures cited in the paper are drawn from Commission forecasts and secondary literature.

## Limitations and conflicts

Both authors are at the University of Amsterdam, Commins as junior lecturer and Irion as associate professor at the Institute for Information Law; the article is open access in a law journal and discloses no external funding or industry involvement. It contributes no energy, water or emissions measurement of its own, and its factual base for sector growth rests on Commission forecasts the authors themselves say are likely to be outpaced by AI-driven buildout. The analysis stops at the point of the 2025 Commission review, so it precedes the first published results of the reporting regime. Being an EU-focused legal reading, it does not transfer to other jurisdictions.

## Relation to existing corpus

Contradicts nothing in policy/019 and sharpens it. Where 019 records that Delegated Regulation (EU) 2024/1364 Article 5(5) keeps facility-level data confidential and Annex IV publishes only aggregates, Commins and Irion reach the same structural conclusion from the legal side and state its consequence: aggregation at member-state and EU level will not make the impacts of specific data centres visible, leaving civil society and local communities unable to identify local effects. They also recommend the Commission assess reported location-based against market-based emissions to increase transparency on RECs, which is the accounting gap quantified in washing/025.
