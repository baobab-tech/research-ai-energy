# Hyperscalers have announced up to 13 GW of nuclear commitments; none of it is new operating capacity, and the earliest restart is targeted for 2027

**Topic:** Data-centre infrastructure — power sourcing, nuclear deals contracted vs delivering
**Source:** Pendleton, J. and Schuessler, M., Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, 2026
**Type:** think-tank report
**URL:** https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/06/beyond-the-hype-assessing-hyperscaler-nuclear-commitments-against-us-energy-realities
**Published:** 2026-06-02

## Finding

Hyperscaler nuclear commitments total up to 13 GW, split roughly evenly between power purchase agreements (~6.9 GW, targeted for the early 2030s) and direct development partnerships (~6.1 GW, targeted mid-2030s and dependent on reactor technologies that have not been commercialised). No new nuclear capacity has been brought online by any of these deals. Even assuming full delivery, the authors calculate the portfolio at roughly 102 TWh/year — under 20% of projected data-centre demand through 2035. The report also identifies the accounting structure that lets an existing reactor be counted as new clean power: in Meta's Clinton agreement, Meta is not buying electricity for its data centres at all. Power continues to be sold and delivered to the regional market; Meta purchases the plant's clean energy credits and counts that generation toward its 100%-renewable target. Nothing about the grid's physical emissions changes.

## Methodology

Assembly and assessment of publicly announced agreements against US energy system constraints. The authors distinguish PPAs on existing or restarting reactors from partnerships contingent on SMRs and other pre-commercial designs, and judge the latter subject to first-of-a-kind manufacturing, licensing, supply-chain and workforce risk.

## Limitations and conflicts

Carnegie Endowment is a policy institute; the report is analysis and judgement, not primary data, and its delivery assessment is qualitative. The 102 TWh/yr figure depends on assumed capacity factors the summary does not state. Announced deal terms are largely as disclosed by the counterparties, so capacity and date figures are self-reported by the firms whose claims are being assessed.

## Corroborating primary evidence on delivery

Two independent checks support the contracted-versus-delivering distinction:
- **Crane/TMI:** Constellation sought FERC assistance with the restart in April 2026 and was granted an NRC waiver in June 2026; NRC environmental review was still taking public comment in July 2026. Target remains 2027. https://www.ans.org/news/2026-04-03/article-7905/constellation-seeks-ferc-help-with-crane-restart/ and https://www.utilitydive.com/news/constellation-three-mile-island-crane-nuclear-ferc-waiver/821836/ (DOE project page: https://www.energy.gov/edf/crane-restart). The NRC reactor record at https://www.nrc.gov/info-finder/reactors/ccec refuses automated retrieval and was not read.
- **Palisades (Holtec, Michigan):** the first US restart of a shut-down reactor, originally targeted for February 2026. As of 2026-07-08 it had still generated no electricity to the grid, with roughly 5,000 work items outstanding and no confirmed restart date; the CEO's stated expectation was to beat a March 2027 contractual supply obligation. https://www.ans.org/news/2026-07-08/article-8187/palisades-restart-projects-holtec-ipo-lawsuit-dismissal-but-no-restart-date/

A restart of an intact, recently-operating plant slipping more than a year past target sets the realistic prior for SMR schedules a decade out.
