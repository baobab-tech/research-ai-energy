# Only 2 of 14 methodological components appear in all five avoided-emissions guidance documents

**Topic:** narrative — is avoided-emissions accounting a coherent category at all?
**Source:** Michelle O'Keeffe and Matthew Brander, University of Edinburgh, 2026
**Type:** peer-reviewed (Journal of Cleaner Production, open access, CC BY)
**URL:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclepro.2026.147672
**Source note:** repository record: https://www.research.ed.ac.uk/en/publications/1e113763-1c70-4946-88b5-95e499b4fac0
**Published:** 2026-02

## Finding

Corporate avoided-emissions disclosures are not comparable because the protocols behind them do not agree on what to calculate. Content analysis of five published guidance documents identified fourteen methodological components; only two appeared in all five. Each of the five documents lacked specificity on at least half of the components it did include, and consistent guidance across documents was observed for only half of the components. The single area of relative alignment — use of a life-cycle approach — still splits on whether that approach should be attributional or consequential, which is the choice that determines whether a counterfactual captures real-world consequences or merely reallocates existing emissions. The authors conclude that "the current set of protocols are unlikely to support comparable disclosures" and call for caution in using avoided-emissions disclosures at all.

## Methodology

Qualitative content analysis, not quantitative modelling. The novelty is comparative: prior academic work assessed individual methodologies in application; this study assesses the guidance documents against each other. Two dimensions are scored per component — whether it is present and defined with specificity, and whether guidance is consistent across documents. The paper does not produce an avoided-emissions estimate of its own, so there is no numerical spread in results to report; the finding is about the protocol landscape.

The attributional/consequential distinction is the load-bearing point for AI claims. An attributional calculation asks what share of emissions is associated with a product; a consequential one asks what would have happened in the world absent the product. Corporate claims such as Google's 41 Mt (see `narrative/022-google-2026-enabled-emissions-41mt.md`) and the IEA's 1 400 Mt scenario (see `narrative/021-iea-2025-energy-and-ai-avoided-emissions-scenario.md`) are presented as consequential but rest on attributional-style reasoning: identify users, attribute the whole physical effect.

## Limitations and conflicts

No grant funding is recorded in OpenAlex; both authors are at the University of Edinburgh Business School. Brander has a prior publication record critical of corporate GHG accounting practice, including the attributional–consequential literature, so the framing is not neutral — but the finding is a structured content analysis whose components and scoring are reproducible, not an opinion piece. The abstract does not name the five documents; the full text (CC BY, available from the Edinburgh repository as `O_KeeffeMBranderMJCP2026AComparisonOfMethodologies.pdf`) is required for that list. The study assesses guidance, not disclosures, so it does not measure how far actual corporate reports diverge in practice.
