# Practitioners who calculate digital-sector carbon describe data centres as opaque systems whose emissions cannot be attributed to a device or service

**Topic:** narrative — why published digital and AI emissions figures disagree, and what the disagreement is made of
**Source:** Gabrielle Samuel (King's College London), Federica Lucivero (University of Oxford), Bran Knowles and Katherine Wright (Lancaster University), 2024
**Type:** peer-reviewed (qualitative interview study)
**URL:** https://doi.org/10.3390/su16052017
**Published:** 2024-02

## Finding

Twenty-four practitioners who research, calculate or set standards for digital-sector carbon emissions were interviewed about how they quantify carbon. They disagreed with each other about the size of the sector's footprint, and the interviews trace that disagreement to specific mechanical obstacles rather than to differing opinion: suppliers release energy data only under contracts forbidding publication, hardware turnover in data centres outruns the peer-review cycle so published calculations are stale on arrival, and the workloads inside a data centre cannot be disentangled to say which device or service caused which draw. One standards-body representative described their own Scope 3 inventory as defensible in four or five categories and invented in about ten. The authors' argument is that the field's standing response to this, a call for more data, treats as temporary a condition that is structural, and that carbon governance has to be built to operate inside the uncertainty instead of waiting for it to close.

## Methodology

Semi-structured interviews with people working across sustainable digitisation. Participants were reached two ways: snowballing from the authors' contacts and from interviewees, and a bibliometric route in which 4,598 Web of Science records from 2016 to 2021 were screened for articles on the environmental impacts of digital technologies, leaving 489, from which VOSViewer generated lists of the most-published and most-cited authors; the top 20 of each list were invited. Transcripts were thematically coded in NVivo 14, with independent broad coding by the first author and a research assistant, merged, then line-by-line coding of the carbon-accounting code. Ethics approval came from the Oxford University Central University Research Ethics Committee (R75723/RE001). The output is coded interview testimony. The study measures no emissions and produces no footprint estimate; the object of measurement is what practitioners can and cannot compute.

## Limitations and conflicts

Funding came from the British Academy, the UK Engineering and Physical Sciences Research Council, the Wellcome Trust, King's College London, and Lancaster University via the Data Science of the Natural Environment project. All four authors are academics in bioethics, social science of digital technology and computing; none declares an industry relationship.

The authors state the sample's limits directly: 24 participants, no low- or middle-income country representation, predominantly male, and mainly European and UK, which they attribute partly to the composition of the literature the bibliometric sampling drew on. The bibliometric route selects the most-published and most-cited authors, which systematically favours established positions. Interviews were conducted in summer 2021, before the generative-AI build-out, so nothing here reflects current data-centre load; what carries forward is the structural account of why attribution inside a data centre is hard, which the intervening hardware growth has not eased. Findings are hypothesis-generating by the authors' own framing and are not tested against any quantitative measure.
