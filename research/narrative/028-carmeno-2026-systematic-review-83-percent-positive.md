# 83% of studies on AI's environmental impact portray it as positive; 11% consider systemic effects

**Topic:** narrative — what does the evidence base on AI and the environment actually contain?
**Source:** Noemi Luna Carmeno, Tiago Domingos, Daniel W. O'Neill, 2026
**Type:** preprint (systematic literature review)
**URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.24091
**Published:** 2026-02-27

## Finding

A systematic review of 1 291 studies selected from 6 655 records finds the literature on AI's environmental impact is both narrow and lopsided. 72% of environmental studies address only energy use and CO2 emissions; 11% consider systemic effects — rebound, induced demand, structural change. 83% portray AI's environmental impact as positive. The contrast with the same review's well-being findings is the check on interpretation: well-being studies split near-evenly (44% positive, 46% negative), so the 83% is not a general optimism bias in AI research but specific to environmental assessment. The authors attribute the skew to scope: assessments that look only at application-level effects, within a narrow boundary and without a life-cycle perspective, find benefits by construction.

## Methodology

Systematic literature review with coded classification of each included study by impact domain, assessment method, and valence of the reported impact. The 83% figure is a count of how studies characterise impact, not a weighted synthesis of effect sizes — it measures what the literature says, not what is true. That is the right measurement for a question about narrative: when a policymaker or journalist samples this literature, five studies in six report a positive environmental impact.

The authors identify three impact levels that a complete assessment requires — computing-related, application-level, and systemic — and find the literature concentrated at the first two. Their recommendations are that environmental assessments incorporate water, material and biodiversity impacts and apply a full life-cycle perspective, and that well-being research move from conceptual to empirical.

## Limitations and conflicts

Preprint, not peer reviewed. Counting study valence is sensitive to the coding scheme and to database coverage, neither of which can be checked from the abstract; the search strategy and inclusion criteria need the full text to assess. A review of 1 291 studies necessarily codes at low resolution. The date range of the included corpus is not stated in the abstract, so the 83% may reflect a literature weighted toward pre-2024 work. Daniel O'Neill (University of Leeds) works in ecological economics and degrowth and has a prior critical of technology-optimist framings; Tiago Domingos is at Instituto Superior Técnico, Lisbon. No funding statement is present in the preprint metadata. The finding is a count with a stated denominator, which limits how far author priors can move it.
