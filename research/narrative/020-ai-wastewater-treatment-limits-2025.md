## Insight

This 2025 review provides a notably balanced assessment of AI applications in wastewater treatment - a sector where AI is frequently promoted as an environmental solution. While acknowledging AI's potential for improved simulation, prediction, control, and adaptation, the paper critically identifies significant barriers to adoption: "laborious instrumentation maintenance, lack of process expertise in the design of current software, instability of control loops, and insufficient incentives for resource efficiency achievement." Importantly, the paper notes that "almost all applications in the sector involve predictive studies, often at a small scale or with limited data use" - suggesting that AI's environmental benefits in this domain remain largely theoretical.

## Identified Barriers to AI Adoption

1. **Instrumentation maintenance** - Laborious upkeep requirements
2. **Software expertise gap** - Lack of process expertise in current AI tool design
3. **Control instability** - AI control loops can be unstable
4. **Insufficient incentives** - No clear motivation for resource efficiency
5. **Data limitations** - Small datasets, limited real-world data

## AI Potential in Wastewater

- Reducing process energy consumption
- Lowering greenhouse gas emissions
- Improving resource recovery
- Superior simulation and prediction
- Adaptive control systems

## Net Impact Implications

This paper's critical tone contrasts with optimistic "AI for environment" narratives:

1. **Benefits remain theoretical**: Real-world AI deployment in wastewater is limited
2. **Implementation barriers are substantial**: Not just technical but organizational and economic
3. **Incentive misalignment**: Even with AI potential, facilities lack motivation for efficiency
4. **Gap between promise and practice**: "Almost all applications" are studies, not deployments

## Critical Observation

The paper's honest assessment - that AI in wastewater is mostly "predictive studies" with "few encouraging but still limited applications" in real systems - represents a more nuanced view than typical AI-for-environment claims. It suggests that translating AI potential into actual environmental benefit requires overcoming significant non-technical barriers.
