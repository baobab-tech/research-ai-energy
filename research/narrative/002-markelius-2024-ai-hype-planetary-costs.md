# AI hype argued to be historically unmatched in planetary and social costs, on secondary evidence only

**Topic:** Narrative and discourse - what drives AI expansion claims
**Source:** Markelius, Wright, Kuiper, Delille & Kuo (Leverhulme Centre for the Future of Intelligence, University of Cambridge), 2024
**Type:** peer-reviewed
**URL:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s43681-024-00461-2
**Published:** 2024-04

## Finding

The paper is a conceptual argument, not a measurement study. It defines AI hype as a global fixation on AI-related technologies, ideas and investments, names four socio-technical mechanisms sustaining it (anthropomorphism, self-proclaimed AI expertise, geopolitical and private-sector fear of missing out, and misappropriation of the term "AI"), and asserts that the present wave is "historically unmatched in terms of magnitude, scale and planetary and social costs" by comparison with earlier AI booms and busts. Every environmental figure in its planetary-costs section is quoted from other sources, several of them news outlets and trade press. The section also states the counter-position at length: after listing data-centre water figures, the authors write that this consumption "is miniscule in comparison to other areas where water is used" and that data-centre water impact "could be argued to not be a significant impact when compared to other sectors' use of water", before arguing that local siting effects still matter.

## Methodology

Argumentative review. The authors compare the current AI boom with the AI winters of the 1970s and late 1980s, identify mechanisms by narrative analysis of press coverage, industry adoption surveys and commentary, and assemble the planetary-costs section from published estimates, journalism and NGO reporting. Adoption evidence is drawn from the IBM Global AI Adoption Index 2022 (35% of businesses using AI, four points above 2021), a UK Charity Digital Skills Report (27 of 100 charities polled), and a figure of 688% growth in UK AI-related businesses over ten years, all cited from their originators. No sampling frame, coding scheme or inter-rater procedure is described for the mechanism analysis.

## Limitations and conflicts

Authors are affiliated with the Leverhulme Centre for the Future of Intelligence, University of Cambridge. Open access under Springer's agreement; no industry funding is declared and no conflict is apparent. The environmental figures are third-hand and several are weak: the 626 billion litre global figure comes from a 2014 newspaper blog post and predates generative AI; the Danish-household comparison and the "100 times an email" ratio are trade-press claims with no stated boundary. The comparison of a per-day US sectoral withdrawal against a per-year global data-centre figure is not like-for-like, and the paper does not correct for that when drawing its comparison. The central claim that the hype is historically unmatched in planetary cost is asserted rather than quantified against the earlier AI periods it invokes.

## Relation to existing corpus

No direct overlap with any measurement in the corpus. The paper's own caveat that data-centre water is small beside agriculture and thermoelectric power sits against the site-level and watershed-level water findings held elsewhere in the corpus, which locate the problem in where and when water is drawn.
