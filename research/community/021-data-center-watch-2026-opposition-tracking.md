## Finding

The two numbers most often repeated about data-centre backlash have different and unequal evidentiary status. Data Center Watch's own tracking is documented: $18 billion of projects blocked and $46 billion delayed over May 2024 - March 2025 across 28 states, with 142 activist groups in 24 states; by March 2026 the group count had risen to 833 (from 396 at end-2025) and Q1 2026 alone saw at least 75 projects worth about $130 billion blocked or delayed. The "cancellations quadrupled in 2025" claim in the RESEARCH_PLAN traces to a research note by Justin Hauke of Baird, an equity research house, reported by Utility Dive and Construction Dive as 25 cancellations in 2025 against 6 in 2024 and 2 in 2023. That note is not public: no title, no publication date, no methodology and no URL are given in any of the secondary coverage. **Treat the quadrupling claim as unverified.**

## Methodology

Data Center Watch states its method plainly: it reviews "local media, government filings, petitions, social media, and official statements" across tracked states to build a database of activist activity and officials involved, updated continuously, and warns that "some figures reflect estimates that may change." Secondary coverage adds that compilation combines large language models with human analysts. Dollar values are announced project values, not capital deployed, and "delayed" has no defined duration threshold, so the $64bn and $130bn figures are counts of announced investment associated with contested projects, not losses.

The report's own caveat is the honest part and should be quoted rather than paraphrased: attributing delays exclusively to opposition "oversimplifies a complex landscape," since projects are also shaped by "regulatory compliance, infrastructure readiness, utility availability, economic incentives, and broader market dynamics."

## Limitations and conflicts

Data Center Watch is funded by 10a Labs, a firm that sells research and analysis to AI companies and US tech firms. A tracker of opposition to data centres, funded by a vendor to the data-centre industry, has an obvious interest in the salience of the problem it measures — though the direction of bias is ambiguous, since the framing ("political risk") is aimed at industry clients rather than at communities. The report explicitly declines to assess the merits of activist concerns.

Counting problems are substantial: no denominator (projects proposed is never given, so a rising block count cannot be distinguished from a rising proposal count), no de-duplication disclosure across quarters, no distinction between a planning-commission continuance and a definitive denial, and a jump from 396 to 833 groups in three months that is at least as likely to reflect improved detection as real growth. The 49-state figure in Q1 2026 versus 24 states in the base report points the same way.

The Baird figure is worse: a paywalled sell-side note, quoted by two trade publications without methodology, with no stated definition of "cancellation." Do not cite it as established.
