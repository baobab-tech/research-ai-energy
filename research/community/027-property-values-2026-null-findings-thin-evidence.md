# Two 2026 analyses find no measurable data-centre penalty on nearby house prices; both have small samples, one was commissioned by a developer seeking approval, and neither is peer-reviewed

**Topic:** Property values and noise — what has actually been measured, and how weak the evidence base is
**Source:** Keith Waters et al., Center for Regional Analysis, George Mason University; Integra Realty Resources (commissioned by Sabey Data Centers), 2026
**Type:** consultancy and university-centre analyses, reported via trade and public media
**URL:** https://www.bisnow.com/national/news/data-center-community-relations/data-centers-may-not-be-a-drag-on-nearby-home-prices-135084
**Source note:** https://www.ipm.org/news/2026-04-13/will-data-centers-impact-property-values-depends-on-who-you-ask
**Published:** 2026-04-13 (IPM) and 2026-06-18 (Bisnow)

## Finding

Property-value loss is among the most frequently cited grounds for opposing data centres, and it is the least evidenced. The two analyses circulating in 2026 both return null or positive results. George Mason University's Center for Regional Analysis examined 2023 Northern Virginia home sales, weighting for industrial and airport proximity, and reports that the analysis "fails to demonstrate statistical evidence that proximity to a data center negatively impacts housing values." Integra Realty Resources, commissioned by Sabey Data Centers while Sabey was seeking approval for an Indiana project, compared 80 homes within 1.5 miles of four Indiana data centres against local markets over 2021-2026: 42 percent appreciation near the facilities versus 41 percent locally. The county-level breakdown undercuts the headline — three of four counties showed homes near data centres gaining 1-9 percentage points *less* than their county average.

## Methodology

Neither is a hedonic study with a credible identification strategy. The GMU analysis is a single cross-section of one year in one market; the IRR analysis is a before-and-after comparison of appreciation rates with n=80 and no controls for house characteristics, and its window largely predates operation of the facilities it is meant to evaluate. Neither uses a quasi-experimental design of the kind used for airport noise (exogenous changes in exposure), and neither is peer-reviewed.

Waters himself supplies the key caveat: Northern Virginia's constrained housing supply may mask a penalty that would appear in markets with more inventory. Terry Clower, also of GMU, warned against generalising: anyone wanting to apply the findings elsewhere should "do so with caution."

## Limitations and conflicts

The IRR analysis was commissioned by a developer for use in a permitting proceeding; its author was unavailable for interview. Both analyses were presented publicly by Sabey Data Centers. That does not make the null result false — a null is a null — but it means no independent party has tried to falsify it, and the absence of any study finding a penalty may reflect who is paying for studies rather than what is true.

Two structural problems with the null results: data centres are often sited in areas that are simultaneously experiencing the growth that raises prices, so a naive comparison is confounded upward; and announcement effects, litigation and rezoning fights occur years before operation, so a 2021-2026 window on facilities not yet running measures nothing about living next to an operating facility.

Conclusion for the corpus: there is currently no reliable evidence either way on data-centre effects on property values. Claims in either direction should be treated as unsupported.
