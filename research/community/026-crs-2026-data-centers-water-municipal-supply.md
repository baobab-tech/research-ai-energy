# Congress's research service finds municipal systems supply ~97% of US data-centre on-site water, that rates and service agreements are largely non-public, and that no comprehensive dataset on data-centre water sources exists

**Topic:** Water-community conflict — who supplies the water, who sets the price, what is disclosed, and where municipalities have refused
**Source:** Congressional Research Service (Folger, Humphreys, Carter, Clark, Gatz, Normand, Stern), 2026
**Type:** agency report (non-partisan legislative branch)
**URL:** https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R49057/R49057.1.pdf
**Source note:** product page: https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R49057
**Published:** 2026-07-31

## Finding

CRS establishes the institutional structure behind data-centre water conflicts rather than new consumption estimates. As of 2025, US data centres met an estimated 97 percent of on-site water needs from municipal public water systems, meaning most disputes are contract disputes with a local utility, not water-rights disputes. The terms of those contracts are systematically opaque: rates charged by public water systems "are not generally publicly available," and water service agreements between a municipality and an operator "may include provisions that limit the disclosure of this information." CRS states flatly that "Comprehensive, publicly available information on data center water sources is not available," and that it could not identify any currently operating US commercial data centre self-sourcing from surface water. It documents one municipality that has refused supply outright: Marana, Arizona, whose Ordinance No. 2024.029 amended the town code so that "The Marana water department will not provide potable water to a data center... for its cooling system, humidity control, and other similar operations."

## Methodology

Secondary synthesis of published studies, state agency materials, local ordinances and local reporting, written for Congress. CRS does not produce original estimates here and is explicit about the provenance and weakness of each number it repeats — including the 97 percent figure, which it attributes to a single source.

The Richland Parish gap is the most useful single data point for methodology generally: the permitted withdrawal (8.4 billion gal/yr) exceeds the operator's stated expected use (500-600 million gal/yr) by roughly fourteenfold. Any analysis using permitted volumes as a proxy for consumption will overstate by an order of magnitude; any analysis using operator self-reports has no independent verification. Both problems recur throughout this corpus.

## Limitations and conflicts

CRS is non-partisan and does not take policy positions, which is its value and its limit: it will not adjudicate between conflicting estimates. The report is structured as FAQs, so it is broad and shallow, and the 17 billion gallon figure carries no boundary description beyond "directly used."

The disclosure finding is the substantive one and it has a consequence for the whole research programme: if water service agreements carry confidentiality clauses and public water system rates are not published, then whether a data centre is paying a cost-reflective rate or being cross-subsidised by residential water customers cannot be determined from public records in most jurisdictions. No quantitative claim about water cost-shifting can currently be supported.
