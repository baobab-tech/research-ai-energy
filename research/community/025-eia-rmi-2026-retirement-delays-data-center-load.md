# US coal retirements fell to 2.6 GW in 2025, the lowest since 2010; utility resource plans now show 4 GW less coal retirement and 8 GW more gas than six months earlier — but the federal data does not attribute this to data centres

**Topic:** Grid emissions dispatch — whether new inflexible load is delaying fossil retirements, and how well that causal claim is actually evidenced
**Source:** US Energy Information Administration, 2026; RMI (Jon Rea), 2026; Evergy IRP update reported by Latitude Media, 2026
**Type:** agency dataset + IRP meta-analysis + utility filing
**URL:** https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=67427
**Source note:** https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=67206 ; https://rmi.org/resources/the-state-of-utility-planning-2026-h1/ ; https://www.latitudemedia.com/news/in-the-midwest-more-coal-power-for-data-centers/
**Published:** EIA 2026-04-13 and 2026-02-23; RMI 2026-07-15; Latitude Media 2026-08-14

## Finding

Retirement delay is measured and large; the attribution to data centres is inferred, not measured. EIA reports 2.6 GW of coal capacity retired in 2025 against 13.7 GW in 2022 — the lowest since 2010 — with operators delaying 4.8 GW of planned 2025 retirements and cancelling 1.1 GW outright, plus 4.8 GW held open by DOE Section 202(c) emergency orders. EIA names the plants and the years but attributes the slowdown to DOE orders and operator decisions, not to load growth or data centres. The attribution appears one level up, in utility resource plans: RMI's review of 23 IRPs covering 10 percent of US generation found, in six months, 4 GW less planned coal retirement, 8 GW more planned gas, a 2-point increase in 2035 load forecasts, and 8 GW less planned wind and solar — pushing projected 2035 emissions from 49 percent to 47 percent below 2005. Evergy's 2026 IRP update, filed with the Kansas Corporation Commission and Missouri PSC, is the concrete case: roughly 2.8 GW of coal deferred, with La Cygne 2, Iatan 1 and Jeffrey 1 removed from the 20-year retirement schedule entirely, after the utility signed agreements for over 2 GW of data centre load.

## Methodology

EIA figures are administrative data reported by generator owners under Form EIA-860M — the closest thing to measurement in this chain. RMI's are a difference between two vintages of planning documents, so they record changes in utility *intentions*, which are strategic documents filed to obtain regulatory approval for capital spending. Evergy's is a single utility's filing in which the utility itself links the deferral to large-load agreements.

The chain of inference runs: load growth is forecast → utilities revise plans → retirements slip. Only the last step is observed in federal data, and EIA offers a competing explanation (DOE emergency orders) for a large share of it. Attributing the 2025 slowdown to data centres specifically requires the IRP layer, which is self-reported.

## Limitations and conflicts

RMI is a clean-energy advocacy and research organisation whose framing treats delayed coal retirement as a setback; its sample is 23 utilities chosen by IRP availability, not randomly, and covers a tenth of generation, so it should not be scaled to the national fleet. Evergy has a direct financial interest in extending the depreciable life of paid-down coal assets and in justifying capital spending, so a data-centre rationale is convenient regardless of whether it is the binding constraint. The Latitude Media report does not give docket numbers; the primary filings at the KCC and Missouri PSC were not retrieved.

The largest single confounder in 2025-2026 is federal policy: DOE 202(c) orders, EPA rule rollbacks and stalled federal approvals for wind and solar all push in the same direction as data-centre load, and no source reviewed separates them.
